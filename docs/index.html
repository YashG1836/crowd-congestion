Who should be told what?

Commuters at a bridge, backups on a network link, diners at a food court, students at the hostel mess: many people share one bottleneck and each one picks when to use it. The rush is a coordination problem, not a capacity problem. We model it as a game, ask what a crowd display should show, and study private advice that people want to follow (a correlated equilibrium). Then we test it on the four messes of IIT Gandhinagar.

What we found

    The two ideas we use

    Nash equilibrium. Nobody can do better by changing only their own choice. It is what selfish timing settles into, and it is costly: each person who joins a queue delays everyone behind them.

    Correlated equilibrium. A mediator (an app) privately tells each person what to do. It is an equilibrium if nobody wants to disobey, given what their own advice reveals. A traffic light is the everyday example.

    Everything on this site is computed by the project's code (docs/data.js is written by experiments.py). All data are synthetic.

    1 · The problem

    One bottleneck, a crowd, and a clock

    Congestion looks different everywhere, but the game is the same: people choose a time slot, a bottleneck serves a fixed number of them per minute, and anyone who arrives when it is busy waits.

    The choice

    12 time slots

    Each person picks one slot (10 minutes at the mess, 15 on the road). Everyone has a favourite time and a flexibility: how many minutes of waiting they would accept to move one slot.

    The bottleneck

    μ people per minute

    A counter, a bridge or a link serves μ people a minute, first come first served. If more arrive, a queue builds and carries into the next slot. Someone who finds q people ahead waits q / μ minutes.

    The cost

    wait + inconvenience

    Your cost is the time you queue plus the inconvenience of not going at your favourite time. Joining a queue delays everyone behind you, but you only feel your own wait.

    Five shared facilities, one model

    Selfish = the Nash equilibrium (found by best-response dynamics). Planner = the cheapest schedule (linear programming + exact local search). Minutes per person per visit.

    3 · Public information

    What does a crowd display do?

    Herding

    Everyone reacts the same way

    Yesterday's quiet slot is today's rush. With a reactive crowd the display multiplies the cost several times in every facility.

    Fewer viewers

    Show it to fewer people

    If only 30% see the display, too few move together to shift the rush. Less shared information can be better.

    Private advice

    Nothing moves

    Private recommendations from a Nash schedule give the same result for every kind of crowd, and nobody switches.

    4 · The main idea

    Advice people want to follow

    The app privately tells each person a slot; inside each group of similar people the slots are shuffled every day. The advice is a correlated equilibrium if nobody can gain by disobeying, given that the others obey. Look at one student's phone at the Jaiswal mess:

    Mess app · today

    This student's cost in every slot, if everyone else follows the app

    Blue = the recommended slot. If it is the lowest bar, obeying is the best choice and the advice is self-enforcing.

    Result 1

    Shuffled Nash advice is a correlated equilibrium

    Every shuffle of a Nash schedule is itself a Nash equilibrium, so obeying is always a best response. Stable from day one; inconvenient slots rotate.

    Result 2

    In a large crowd advice alone cannot beat Nash

    With complete information every correlated equilibrium of a large crowd costs exactly what Nash costs. Our linear program confirms it in all five facilities.

    Result 3

    A small reward reaches the optimum

    The correlated-equilibrium conditions give the smallest reward that makes the planner's schedule self-enforcing. Nobody ends up worse off than under selfish timing.

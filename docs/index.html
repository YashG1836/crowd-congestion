Who should be told what?

Cars at a bridge, backups on a network, diners at a food court, students at the hostel mess: many people share one bottleneck and each picks when to use it. The rush is a coordination problem, not a capacity problem. We model it as a game, ask what a crowd display should show, and use correlated equilibrium (private advice people want to follow) to fix it. Then we test everything on the four IITGN messes.

1 · The problem

One model, many crowded places

Each place is the same game: n people, 12 time slots, one bottleneck serving μ people a minute. Your cost = time in the queue + inconvenience of going away from your favourite time. Every person who joins a queue delays everyone behind them, but only feels their own wait. So selfish choices (a Nash equilibrium) cost far more than the schedule a planner would pick.

2 · Three small games, solved exactly

The whole idea in three tiny games

Small enough to solve by hand, and they carry the whole story: selfishness wastes, shared information can backfire, and private correlated advice can do better than any Nash equilibrium.

Price of anarchy

Pigou's two roads

Road A always costs 1. Road B costs xp if a share x uses it. Selfishly everyone takes B. A planner splits the traffic.

Beyond Nash

A traffic light

Two drivers: Stop or Go. Payoffs: both stop 6/6, one goes 7 vs 2, both go = crash 0/0.

The best correlated equilibrium (a linear program) says: ½ both stop, ¼ each goes alone. Each driver gets 5.25, more than any mixture of Nash equilibria (4.67). It never recommends a crash.

5 · The main idea

Advice people want to follow

The app privately tells each student a slot. Inside each group of similar students the slots are shuffled every day. The advice is a correlated equilibrium if nobody can gain by disobeying, given that everyone else obeys. Pick what the app recommends and look at one student's phone:

Proposition 1

Shuffled Nash = correlated equilibrium

Every shuffle of a Nash schedule is itself a Nash equilibrium, so obeying is always a best response. Stable from day one, and inconvenient slots rotate.

Proposition 2

Advice alone cannot beat Nash

6 · Same problem, other lenses

Live counter, learning, uncertainty

MDP

Go now or wait? (within one evening)

With a live counter, a ready student can wait for the queue to shrink. This is a Markov decision process; value iteration gives a threshold policy. A fresh counter helps everyone; a stale one makes students rush together.

no displayeveryone, fresh countereveryone, 5-min-old counter
MAB · RL

Students who learn (300 dinners)

Bandit learners only see their own wait; full-information learners see every slot's wait (if the app publishes it). Swap regret 0 means their play is a correlated equilibrium.

Extensive form

A counter that shows earlier arrivals

If student 2 sees student 1's choice, the game becomes sequential. Backward induction gives the subgame-perfect equilibrium (Peak, Late): efficient, but the first mover gets the better slot. A Nash equilibrium with the threat "I will go at the peak anyway" exists but is not credible.
